Behind Victoria’s road toll

Who is hurt on our roads, when crashes happen, and where the risk is highest, 2012–2024

In 2024, 284 people died on Victoria’s roads, more than five every week. Thousands more were seriously injured.

Each square is one person killed, grouped by how they were travelling. Drag the slider to compare any year since 2012.

The toll is not falling

Victoria lost 211 people in 2020, when lockdowns kept cars off the road. By 2023 the toll had climbed back to 295, the highest since this dataset begins in 2012.

Regional Victoria is home to about one in four Victorians, yet it accounts for more than half of all road deaths.

Darker cells mean more crashes. The pale cells of 2020 and 2021 are Melbourne’s COVID-19 lockdowns: in April 2020 crashes fell by more than 40% compared with April 2019.

Within months of each lockdown ending, crash numbers were back where they had been.

Without a steel shell, a crash is far more serious

Drivers make up two thirds of everyone caught up in injury crashes, and about half of them walk away unhurt.

For people outside a car the outcome is very different: 46% of motorcyclists and 42% of pedestrians involved in a crash were killed or seriously injured.

A serious injury generally means the person was admitted to hospital.

Crashes follow the commute. Night crashes are the deadliest.

Crash numbers rise with weekday traffic: the morning school run and the evening peak from 3pm to 6pm.

Switch to fatal crashes and the commute pattern fades. Afternoons are still busy, but the early hours of Saturday and Sunday see about twice as many deaths as the same hours on weekdays.

Fewer crashes happen at night, but each one is far more likely to kill, as the clock below shows.

The risk is highest in the regions

Measured against the number of people who live there, serious crashes are most common in rural shires crossed by busy highways and tourist routes, such as Murrindindi, Towong and Strathbogie. Per resident, a fatal crash is about three and a half times as likely in Regional Victoria as in Melbourne.

Speed decides how bad a crash is

On roads signed 100 km/h, about 1 in 20 injury crashes kills someone. On 60 km/h suburban roads it is about 1 in 89.

Higher speed limits are mostly on rural roads, which is a large part of why the regions carry so much of the toll.

What the numbers add up to

Where you drive matters

One in four Victorians lives in the regions, but more than half of road deaths happen there.

Speed and darkness multiply harm

A crash on a 100 km/h road is about four times as likely to kill as one on a 60 km/h road. The same gap separates crashes after midnight from those in the morning peak.

Unprotected road users carry more of the toll

Motorcyclist deaths rose from 41 in 2012 to 61 in 2024, and pedestrian deaths are also up.
